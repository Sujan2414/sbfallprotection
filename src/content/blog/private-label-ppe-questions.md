Distributors come to us at two points: when they want to add a private-label range, and
when the supplier they already had has let them down. The second conversation is
usually more instructive.

If your name is on the label, the buyer holds you responsible — for the certification,
the consistency and the paperwork. Here is what to establish before you place the
first order.

## 1. Do you manufacture, or do you buy and relabel?

The most important question, and the easiest to dodge. A trading house can show you the
same photographs and the same certificates as a factory. The difference appears when
something needs changing or something goes wrong.

Ask for a video walkthrough of the production floor, unedited, showing the operation
you are buying: webbing cutting, stitching, hardware assembly, finishing, packing. Ask
who owns the machines. Ask what happens in-house and what is subcontracted.

## 2. Which certification, and can I see the type-examination file?

A CE mark is a claim. The type-examination documentation from a notified body is the
evidence behind it. Fall protection is PPE Category III, which means ongoing notified
body involvement — not a self-declaration.

Ask for the certificate **and** the file it refers to, and check that the product
described matches the product you are buying. Confirm the standard's edition, not just
the number.

## 3. What is tested, and how often?

There is a large gap between "the design passed type testing once" and "every batch is
verified". Ask what is tested on incoming materials, what is tested in process, and what
is tested on finished goods. Ask whether test records are retained per batch, and
whether you can have them with your shipment.

## 4. How is my batch traceable?

If a problem emerges in the field, you need to know which units are affected without
recalling everything you ever sold. Ask how serial or batch numbers are assigned,
what they encode, and whether the manufacturer can tell you — two years later — which
webbing lot and which production run a given serial came from.

## 5. What can actually be customised, and what cannot?

Branding, webbing colour, packaging and labelling are usually straightforward. Changes
to load-bearing geometry, hardware or stitch patterns are a different matter: they can
invalidate the type examination and require re-certification. A good manufacturer will
tell you which of your requests fall on which side of that line, and will not agree to
everything.

## 6. What are the MOQs — per SKU or per order?

This is where private-label programmes quietly become expensive. A low headline MOQ
across an order can still mean high minimums per colour, per size and per
configuration. Get it in writing at SKU level, and model what it means for slow-moving
variants sitting in your warehouse.

## 7. What is the real lead time, including certification and artwork?

Production time is only part of it. Add artwork approval, label proofs, sampling,
pre-production approval and shipping. Ask for the timeline for a **first** order
separately from repeat orders — the first is always longer, and unrealistic
expectations here damage your own customer relationships.

## 8. Who owns the tooling, artwork and design?

If you paid for tooling or contributed design work, establish who owns it and whether
you can take it elsewhere. Establish too whether your private-label design can be sold
to anyone else — including your competitor down the road.

## 9. What happens when something is wrong?

Ask directly: what is the process for a defect claim, who pays for the return, what are
the timelines, and can you speak to someone who will make a decision rather than relay
one. Then ask for a reference from a distributor who has actually had a problem. How a
supplier behaves on a bad day tells you more than a factory tour does.

## Two things worth doing before the first full order

**Order a sample and destroy it.** Cut it open. Look at the stitching from the inside,
the hardware finish, the webbing edges. It costs very little and tells you a lot.

**Start smaller than you want to.** A first order sized to be survivable if it goes
wrong buys you real information about lead times, consistency and communication before
you have serious money committed.
