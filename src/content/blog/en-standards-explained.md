Buyers often ask us for "EN certified harnesses". It is a reasonable request, but the
certification does not work quite like that. In the European system each **component**
is certified against the standard for that component, and the system only works when
the parts are compatible with each other and with the way the work is done.

Here is what the common numbers cover.

## The standards you will meet most often

**EN 361 — Full body harnesses.** The only body-holding device permitted for fall
arrest. Defines the attachment points that may be used to arrest a fall, marked with
a capital **A**. If a point is not marked A, it is not a fall arrest point.

**EN 354 — Lanyards.** The connecting element itself. Important: a lanyard certified
to EN 354 alone is a *connecting* component. It is not a fall arrest lanyard unless
combined with energy absorption.

**EN 355 — Energy absorbers.** The part that limits the force transmitted to the body
during an arrest. This is the standard that caps arrest force at a maximum of **6 kN**.

**EN 360 — Retractable type fall arresters.** Self-retracting lifelines that pay out
and retract as the worker moves and lock when a fall begins.

**EN 362 — Connectors.** Karabiners, snap hooks and scaffold hooks, including gate
strength and locking requirements.

**EN 795 — Anchor devices.** The certified point the whole system connects to.

**EN 358 — Work positioning systems.** Belts and lanyards that hold a worker in
position at the work face. **Not fall arrest.** A positioning system supports a worker
so their hands are free; it is not designed or certified to arrest a fall, and it must
be backed by a separate fall arrest system where a fall is possible.

**EN 341 — Descender devices.** Controlled descent for rescue.

**EN 1496 — Rescue lifting devices.** For raising or lowering a person during rescue.

## Why a harness on its own is not a system

A compliant fall arrest system needs four things, and the acronym **ABCD** is the
easiest way to remember it:

- **A — Anchorage.** A certified point of adequate strength, correctly positioned.
- **B — Body support.** A full body harness to EN 361.
- **C — Connection.** The lanyard, energy absorber or retractable block that joins the
  two, and the connectors at each end.
- **D — Descent and rescue.** The plan and equipment to get a suspended person down.

Miss any of the four and you do not have a system, however good the individual parts
are. The last one is the one most often missing, and it is the subject of
[its own article](/blog/suspension-trauma-rescue-plan).

## Reading a label properly

A compliant label should let you answer all of these:

1. **Which standard, and which year?** "EN 361:2002" is a specific edition, not a
   generic claim.
2. **Who made it, and when?** Manufacturer and date of manufacture, which starts the
   service-life clock.
3. **Which serial or batch?** This is what ties the item to your inspection records.
4. **Where are the A points?** On a harness, the fall arrest attachments must be
   identifiable.
5. **Is there a CE or UKCA mark, with the notified body number** where the category
   requires it? Fall protection is PPE Category III, which requires ongoing involvement
   of a notified body — not a self-declaration.

If a supplier offers a "CE certificate" but cannot produce the type-examination
documentation behind it, treat that as unresolved rather than settled.

## Compatibility is your responsibility, not the standard's

Two certified components can still make an unsafe combination. The classic examples:

- A **snap hook clipped to a wide anchor bar or a D-ring it does not fit** can be
  loaded across the gate rather than along the spine. Gate-face loading is far weaker
  than the rated axial strength.
- **Connecting two lanyards together** to gain length adds an uncertified link and
  changes the fall distance the system was designed around.
- Using a **positioning lanyard as a fall arrest lanyard** because it happened to be
  on the belt.

The standards certify parts. Assembling those parts into a system that suits the
actual task, the actual anchor and the actual clearance available is an engineering
decision on site.

## What this means when you are buying

Ask for the standard *and* the edition, the type-examination documentation, and the
manufacturer's instructions for use — the instructions are where compatibility limits
and inspection intervals live. A supplier who manufactures rather than resells should
be able to produce all three without hunting.

---

*Standards are revised over time and requirements differ between jurisdictions —
EN in Europe, ANSI/OSHA in the United States, IS in India. This article is an
orientation, not a compliance document. Confirm the current requirements that apply
to your site and always follow the manufacturer's instructions supplied with the
equipment.*
