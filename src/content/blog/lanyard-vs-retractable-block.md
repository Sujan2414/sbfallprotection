The **C** in the ABCD of fall protection — the connection between harness and anchor —
is where most specification decisions are actually made. Two options dominate:
an energy-absorbing lanyard, or a retractable type fall arrester, commonly called a
self-retracting lifeline or block.

They are not interchangeable.

## The core difference

An **energy-absorbing lanyard** is a fixed length of webbing or rope with an absorber
in line. It hangs slack. In a fall the worker free-falls the slack, then the absorber
tears open to limit the force.

A **retractable block** keeps the line under light tension at all times, paying out and
retracting as the worker moves. In a fall it locks quickly, like a seatbelt, so free
fall is short.

That single difference — slack versus tension — drives everything else.

## Clearance

This is usually the deciding factor. Because a lanyard allows real free fall before the
absorber engages, a lanyard system needs substantial clearance below the anchor: with a
2 m lanyard, commonly around 6 m once absorber deployment, worker height and a safety
margin are added.

A block arrests over a much shorter distance, so it will often work where a lanyard
cannot fit at all. If you are working at intermediate height — a few metres up, with a
floor or obstruction below — the block is frequently the only workable choice. The
[fall clearance calculation](/blog/fall-clearance-calculation) is worth running before
you decide.

## Mobility

A block's line follows the worker and stays out of the way, which suits climbing and
work over a wider area. A lanyard is simpler but leaves slack that snags, and workers
have to manage it.

Where a worker must move continuously without ever disconnecting, a **twin-leg**
(forked) lanyard has the advantage: one leg stays attached while the other is moved to
the next anchor, giving genuine 100% tie-off. Blocks generally do not offer that on
their own — you would need two.

## Cost, weight and maintenance

Lanyards are far cheaper, lighter, have nothing mechanical to fail, and are simple to
inspect visually. Blocks cost significantly more, are heavier at the anchor, contain a
mechanism, and typically require periodic servicing by the manufacturer or an
authorised centre in addition to routine inspection.

For a large workforce this matters. Kitting out a hundred workers with blocks is a very
different budget from a hundred lanyards, and the servicing obligation is ongoing.

## Where each one is the wrong choice

**Do not use a lanyard when:**
- Clearance below is limited
- The anchor cannot be placed above the worker
- Continuous movement means slack will constantly snag

**Do not use a block when:**
- It is anchored at or below foot level and is not specifically rated for that. Most
  are designed for overhead anchorage.
- It would be used over a sharp or leading edge without being rated for edge use. A
  standard block's line can be cut over an edge during a fall.
- It is dragged horizontally so the line rubs across structure.

## A rule of thumb, and its limits

Broadly: **limited clearance or lots of movement points to a block; generous clearance,
overhead anchor and tight budget points to a lanyard; the need for continuous
attachment while relocating points to a twin-leg lanyard.**

That is a starting point, not a specification. The right answer depends on the actual
anchor position, the actual clearance, the edges present, and how the work is done.
Where the choice is not obvious, involve a competent person — and tell your supplier
what the work looks like rather than only what product you think you want. We are
often able to suggest a configuration that solves the problem more cheaply than the
one originally asked for.
