A full body harness is the only thing between a worker and the ground. It is also
the piece of equipment most likely to be thrown in the back of a van, dragged across
a concrete slab, and left in the sun for a season. Inspection is not paperwork — it
is the step that catches the damage those things cause.

There are two inspections that matter: the **pre-use check**, done by the wearer
before every use, and the **periodic inspection**, done by a competent person at
intervals your risk assessment defines. This article covers the first one. It takes
about a minute once the habit is built.

## Work through the harness in one direction

The most common mistake is checking the parts you can see and missing the parts you
cannot. Start at one shoulder and work the whole harness in a single continuous loop
so nothing is skipped: shoulder straps, chest strap, dorsal area, waist, thigh straps,
and back to the start.

Run the webbing through your hands as you go, bending it into an inverted **U** every
few inches. Flexing the webbing opens the weave and makes damage visible that lies
flat and hidden under tension.

## What you are looking for on the webbing

- **Cuts, nicks and frayed edges.** Any cut across load-bearing webbing takes the
  harness out of service. There is no "small enough" cut.
- **Abrasion and fuzzing.** Light surface fuzz from normal wear is expected. Fuzzing
  that has thinned the webbing, or worn away the pattern, is not.
- **Heat, sparks and chemical damage.** Look for glazed, shiny, hard or brittle
  patches. Welding spatter leaves hard glossy spots. Chemical exposure can leave
  webbing discoloured, stiff or unusually soft.
- **UV degradation.** Sun-bleached, chalky webbing that has lost colour has usually
  lost strength with it. A harness stored on a dashboard or hung outdoors ages fast.
- **Paint, tar or heavy soiling.** Contamination hides damage underneath and can
  stiffen fibres. If you cannot see the webbing, you cannot inspect it.

## Stitching

Load-bearing stitch patterns are your primary structural connections. Look for
**pulled, cut, broken or missing stitches**, and for stitching that has begun to
loosen at the ends of a pattern. Compare each stitch block against an identical one
elsewhere on the harness — the difference is easier to see than the defect itself.

## Hardware

D-rings, buckles, adjusters and keepers should be checked for:

- Cracks, deformation, sharp edges or burrs
- Distortion — a D-ring that is no longer flat and true
- Corrosion or pitting beyond light surface discolouration
- Buckles that do not engage positively, or adjusters that slip under load
- Excessive wear where webbing passes through metal

Work every buckle. A quick-connect buckle should click home cleanly and resist a
firm pull. If it needs coaxing, it fails.

## The fall indicator

Most modern harnesses carry a fall arrest indicator — commonly a folded and stitched
section of webbing that tears open, or a marked tell-tale, when the harness has taken
an arrest load. **A deployed indicator means the harness is finished.** It does not
matter how good the rest of it looks.

## Labels

If the labels are missing, illegible or unreadable, the harness cannot be verified as
compliant and should be removed from service. The label carries the standard it was
certified to, the manufacturing date, and the serial or batch number that ties it to
its inspection record.

## Findings that permanently retire a harness

Some findings are not repairable and not a judgement call:

1. The harness has arrested a fall, or the fall indicator has deployed
2. Any cut, tear or burn to load-bearing webbing or stitching
3. Cracked, deformed or badly corroded hardware
4. Chemical damage, or heat damage that has glazed or embrittled the webbing
5. Missing or illegible labels
6. It has passed the manufacturer's stated maximum service life

Do not return a failed harness to the store where someone else can pick it up. Tag it
clearly, cut the webbing so it cannot be reused, and dispose of it.

## Build the habit, and record it

The check is only useful if it happens every time. Two things make that stick: keep
harnesses on a rack rather than in a heap, so damage is visible at pick-up, and keep
a simple inspection log per serial number. When an auditor asks how you know a harness
is fit for use, the log is the answer.

---

*This guidance covers general good practice. Always follow the inspection instructions
supplied with your specific equipment, and the requirements of the standards and
regulations that apply in your jurisdiction. If you are unsure whether a harness is
fit for service, take it out of service.*
